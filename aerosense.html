Catch what
breath carries.

Most drugs don't evaporate. They leave the lungs inside microscopic droplets of airway fluid. AeroSense is a handheld reader designed to catch those droplets on a disposable test strip, read them electrochemically, and give a Reference, Abnormal or Inconclusive result in under a minute, fully offline.

The science

Why we catch droplets instead of sniffing gas.

Alcohol is volatile, so it evaporates into breath as a gas and a sensor can "smell" it. Drugs like THC, opioids and amphetamines are heavy, non-volatile molecules. They don't become gas. They leave the body a different way.

No gas

Drugs aren't volatile

THC, opioids and amphetamines are heavy molecules. Unlike alcohol, they don't evaporate into breath, so a gas sensor can't smell them.

0.5–5µm

They ride out in droplets

The fluid lining the deep airways exchanges compounds with the blood, then breaks into microscopic droplets with every breath.

0needles

A sample without a needle

Catch those droplets and you have a non-invasive sample of what's circulating. Prof. Olof Beck's research has shown drugs are present in this aerosol after use.

Let physics sort the breath.

A nozzle speeds the breath up and points it at the test strip. Gas turns sharply and escapes, but heavier droplets can't turn in time, so they hit the strip. Move the sliders to see which droplet sizes get caught.

Anatomy

Take it apart, one layer at a time.

Keep scrolling. The model follows along.

01 · the reader

One hand, one breath.

A palm-sized reader with a screen and three result lights. Everything that touches the sample lives in the cartridge on top.

02 · the cartridge

Single-use, zero carryover.

Mouthpiece, impactor nozzle, buffer blister and electrode strip come as one sealed cartridge. It clicks in, and it's thrown away after one test.

03 · the capture

Droplets down, gas out.

Breath turns sharply above the strip. Droplets keep going and land on the electrode; gas escapes through the side vents. The chamber is held at 37 °C.

04 · the electronics

A lab bench on one board.

A flow sensor checks the breath, a potentiostat reads the strip, and an ESP32 runs the classifier. An 18650 cell powers a full shift.

05 · the answer

A result an officer can act on.

The curve is compared against a reference on the device itself, and one of three lights comes on. No lab, no network.

What's new

The science exists. Pocket-sized, it doesn't yet.

Breath-aerosol collectors already exist for research, but samples are sealed and mailed to a lab for mass spectrometry. Companies like Hound Labs have shown breath-based THC testing is commercially viable, with premium hardware. Our aim is to bring capture and analysis together in one low-cost handheld device.

Demo plan

Proving it safely, without a single controlled substance.

Testing with real narcotics isn't legal or safe outside authorised forensic labs under the NDPS Act. So our demo proves the full physical and electrochemical pipeline using safe surrogates.

01

Breath-sized mist

A medical mesh nebulizer makes a 1–5 µm saline aerosol, the same size range as exhaled breath aerosol.

02

Visible impaction

A dye tracer in the mist shows the droplets landing on the electrode while air leaves through the exhaust.

03

Measurable shift

A clean sample and a surrogate "biomarker" sample produce clearly different curves, and the device classifies each one live.

our answer to "did you test real drugs?"

For real drugs, the capture, measurement and classification stay exactly the same. Only the receptor on the electrode changes, developed and validated with an authorised lab partner.

What the prototype will prove: aerosol capture, the electrochemical readout, and on-device classification of the signal. What comes later: drug-specific detection limits, which need validated receptors and controlled clinical trials.
